Product Blueprint

Quant Advisory Terminal

A disciplined trading assistant. It watches a list of shares, proposes trades against written rules, and refuses far more often than it acts — then records why, every time.

Mode Paper — no real money Market US equities Broker Alpaca (paper) Phase Validation

What it is, and what it is not

It is a rule-keeper. The trading ideas themselves are ordinary and public — the value is that every idea is forced through the same checks, in the same order, with the same limits, and nothing is skipped because it looked obvious at the time.

It is not a money machine. Nothing here predicts prices. The system is currently being tested to answer one question: does this approach make money after costs, often enough to be worth running? That question is still open, and the honest answer today is that there is not yet enough evidence to say.

The single most important idea in this document: the app and the broker are two different places. Anything that must survive a power cut, a crash, or the app simply being closed has to live at the broker — and the protective sell orders do.

How a trade actually happens

Seven stages, in this order, every time. A candidate that fails any stage stops there and the reason is written down.

Price data arrives In the app

Live prices for about 100 shares. If a share stops trading for 15 minutes it is set aside rather than acted on — a stale price is worse than no price.

The market's mood is classified In the app

A statistical model sorts conditions into four states — calm, rising, falling, volatile — and sets an exposure dial between 0 and 1. In a defensive state every position is sized smaller, automatically.

A strategy raises its hand In the app

One strategy is live: swing. It looks for shares in an established uptrend. It only ever suggests; it cannot size or place anything.

The trade is sized against a stop In the app

Before deciding how many shares, the system decides where it would admit it was wrong — 2.5× the share's recent daily range below entry. The number of shares is then whatever puts 1% of the account at risk if that level is reached. Bigger swings mean fewer shares, not more risk.

Every limit is checked In the app

Eleven separate gates, listed overleaf. Most trim the order down to what fits rather than rejecting it outright. If nothing fits, it is refused and the reason recorded.

A human signs it off Person

Nothing reaches the broker without an explicit, named approval. This is the one rule with no exceptions. (An opt-in setting can delegate this to the system for a named strategy — see Three brakes.)

It is sent, with its exits attached At the broker

The buy goes out bundled with two sell orders — a protective stop below and a profit target above. They are linked: if one fills, the other is cancelled. They sit at the broker indefinitely, whether or not the app is running.

Where the protection actually lives

This is the distinction worth insisting on, because it decides what happens on the worst day.

In the app — stops when the app stops

  • Deciding position size
  • Checking every gate
  • Watching for lost protection, every 5 minutes
  • Comparing its own records against the broker's
  • The kill switch

At the broker — works regardless

  • The protective stop on every position
  • The profit target paired with it
  • Cancelling one when the other fills
  • Refusing to sell shares that aren't there

If the computer is switched off overnight, every position still has a working stop sitting at the broker. When the app restarts it asks the broker what is actually resting — it never assumes its own memory is right — and proposes a replacement for anything missing.

How the AI is used

The AI is a commentator, not a trader. It explains context in plain language: what the wider market is doing, what a company's fundamentals look like, and how a period's results read.

  • ✕It cannot place an order.
  • ✕It cannot decide how large a position should be.
  • ✕It cannot approve, override, or relax any limit above.

Every number the AI discusses is calculated in ordinary code first and handed to it as settled fact. It narrates figures; it never produces them. Anything fetched from outside — news, commentary — is passed in clearly labelled as untrusted, so instructions hidden inside it cannot become instructions to the system.

A deliberate limitation. Because the AI cannot affect a decision, it also cannot be blamed for one. If a trade goes wrong, the reason is in the rules and the recorded inputs — not in what a language model said that day.

Three brakes

Sign-off

No order reaches the broker without a named approval. Auto-approval can be switched on for one named strategy at a time, on paper only, and it is off unless deliberately enabled.

Kill switch

If the app's record of what is held ever disagrees with the broker's, all new orders halt until a person looks. Disagreement means something is not understood — and acting on a misunderstanding is the expensive kind of mistake.

The freeze

While the system is being evaluated, no change is permitted that would alter which trades happen or how large they are — however obviously right it looks. A result has to belong to one version of the system, or it means nothing. Fixing faults and recording more detail remain allowed.

How you would know it works

A strategy is not considered proven until it has produced 30 completed trades with a positive average result, a win rate above 40%, and no single loss larger than three times the average win. Until then it is marked as unproven on screen, even while it trades.

Every completed trade is recorded with the price paid, the price received, the costs, how far it moved against the position before resolving, and why it ended. Every refusal is recorded with the rule that stopped it. The point is that in three months there is an evidence file, not an impression.

Where it stands today. One completed trade — a loss of $482, closed by its protective stop exactly as designed. Ten positions open, all protected. The system is refusing roughly nineteen candidates for every one it accepts, almost always because the ten-position limit is already full.
